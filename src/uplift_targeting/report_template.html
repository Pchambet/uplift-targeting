E-mail the customers the e-mail moves, not the ones who would buy anyway

A retailer e-mailed {{N}} customers at random: a Mens campaign, a Womens campaign, or nothing. The question a campaign team faces is whom to e-mail, and with which e-mail, so that the campaign earns money it would not have earned anyway. This page answers it with out-of-sample, uncertainty-aware estimates, and checks the method at scale on {{CRITEO_ROWS}} advertising users.

On this experiment, no targeting rule beats e-mailing everyone

Decision memo

  1. Send the Mens e-mail. It lifts conversion by {{MENS_CONV_PP}}, 95% CI {{MENS_CONV_CI}}, and spend by {{MENS_SPEND}} per customer, about twice the Womens e-mail ({{WOMENS_SPEND}}).
  2. Send it to everyone while an e-mail costs less than about {{BREAK_EVEN_COST}} all-in. At {{COST_FMT}} it earns {{BLANKET_VALUE}} per 1,000 customers, 95% CI {{BLANKET_CI}}; the best uplift procedure earns {{UPLIFT_VALUE}}, CI {{UPLIFT_CI}}, a paired difference of {{UPLIFT_MINUS_BLANKET}}, CI {{UPLIFT_MINUS_BLANKET_CI}}.
  3. Do not trust an in-sample model contest. Picking the best of 30 rankings and budgets on the evaluation data would have reported {{NAIVE_VALUE}} per 1,000, {{NAIVE_GAIN}} over blanket sending. The gain is selection noise.
  4. Do not assume an uplift model beats a response model: test it on the experiment. Here the response model ranked as well as or better than every meta-learner, on Hillstrom and on {{CRITEO_ROWS}} Criteo users, where its top 20% holds {{CRITEO_TOP20_RESPONSE_SHARE}} of all incremental conversions against {{CRITEO_TOP20_DR_SHARE}} for the DR-learner. Uplift learners only help where the effect is decoupled from the baseline, as with the Womens e-mail, and even there the gain is within noise.

Pre-declared segments

Five segment dimensions were declared before any model was fitted and tested once per e-mail and outcome: 30 heterogeneity tests, Holm-corrected. {{HOLM_SURVIVORS}} survive, both on visits and both on product line: the Womens e-mail barely moves customers who only ever bought menswear. Purchases and spend show no detectable heterogeneity at this sample size.

BCan a model find the persuadable customers?

Five meta-learners (S, T, X, cross-fitted DR, transformed outcome), each with a LightGBM and a regularised linear base model, trained with five-fold cross-fitting so that every customer is scored by models that never saw them, and benchmarked against the response model a marketing team would build.

Where heterogeneity exists, the models find it: for the Womens e-mail, the DR-learner's Qini coefficient on visits is {{QINI_WOMENS_VISIT}} (×1,000, bootstrap 95% CI), clearly above random. But the plain response model finds most of it too ({{QINI_WOMENS_VISIT_RESPONSE}}); the paired difference, DR-learner minus response, is {{QINI_DIFF_WOMENS_VISIT}}. For the Mens e-mail the DR-learner is no better than random ({{QINI_MENS_VISIT}}) and the response model ranks better ({{QINI_DIFF_MENS_VISIT}}): that e-mail's effect grows with the customer's baseline propensity. On spend, {{SPEND_QINI_SIGNIFICANT}} rankings have a Qini interval that excludes zero.

Thirty candidate rankings compete: five learners and two base models, trained on visits, conversions or spend. A model trained on dense visits might rank customers better for revenue than one trained on sparse revenue, so the choice is left to data, but to data the final score never sees. The candidates' merit barely replicates from one random half of the customers to the other: rank correlation {{RHO_SPEND}} on spend, against {{RHO_VISIT}} on visits. With 578 buyers, the best-looking model is mostly the luckiest one.

DWhat changes when the experiment is two hundred times larger?

The Criteo Uplift v2.1 benchmark logs {{CRITEO_ROWS}} users from randomised advertising incrementality tests, 85% treated. The same learners were fitted on a hash-based sample of {{CRITEO_TRAIN}} users from one half and evaluated on all {{CRITEO_TEST}} users of the other half. Treatment lifts visits by {{CRITEO_ATE_VISIT}} and conversions by {{CRITEO_ATE_CONV}} on average.

At this size every ranking beats random by a wide margin, and the effect is concentrated: the top 10% of users by the response model hold {{CRITEO_TOP10_VISIT_SHARE}} of all incremental visits. Their untreated visit rate is {{CRITEO_TOP_DECILE_BASE}} and treatment adds {{CRITEO_TOP_DECILE_LIFT}}: here the effect tracks the baseline rate, so ranking by "who will act" is also ranking by "who will be moved". The response model is the best uplift ranker (Qini ×1,000 on conversions: {{CRITEO_QINI_CONV_RESPONSE}}, against {{CRITEO_QINI_CONV_T}} for the T-learner and {{CRITEO_QINI_CONV_DR}} for the DR-learner). The meta-learners pay for estimating a small difference between two large, noisy predictions; with 15% controls and 0.3% conversions, the DR-learner's final stage even returns the same score for {{CRITEO_DR_TIE}} of users.

Method and limits

  • Out-of-sample by construction. Five outer folds; every score comes from models fitted without that customer. The DR-learner cross-fits its own nuisance models inside the training folds. A test scrambles held-out outcomes and checks that held-out scores do not move.
  • Known propensities. Assignment probabilities are fixed by design (1/3 per arm), so IPW and doubly robust policy values are unbiased without a propensity model. Tests check both on synthetic data, the DR one with a deliberately wrong outcome model.
  • Selection is part of the model. Ranking and budget are chosen on one random half and applied to the other; the two halves are pooled.
  • Uncertainty. Qini intervals are a Poisson bootstrap over customers with scores held fixed: they cover evaluation noise, not retraining noise. Policy intervals are normal approximations on per-customer doubly robust scores.
  • Assumed economics. The data has no margins or costs. The defaults ({{MARGIN_FMT}} gross margin, {{COST_FMT}} per e-mail including fatigue and unsubscribe risk) are assumptions; the sliders show the conclusions under others.
  • Limits. One two-week campaign from 2008 with 578 buyers; spend is heavy-tailed. Criteo features are anonymised, its labels are visits and conversions (not revenue), and its Qini intervals use 20 random groups of the held-out half instead of a bootstrap. A multiplicative model (response × relative lift) was not tried and would likely close the Criteo gap.
